# metaskills and more for computational research

(This README is an overview of this repo. I would suggest reading at least the intro to learn what the repo is for, but. If you are overwhelmed by all the things in this repo and don't know where to start, look at the `aaaaa-help` folder!)

Computational researchers need a lot of meta-skills that don't ever get taught formally: technical skills like using a shell/command-line utilities, running jobs on high-performance clusters, and writing clean, extensible, and publishable code; communication skills like writing papers, making figures, and presenting research orally; scientific project management skills like reading papers effectively, identifying research directions, and setting milestones. 

The prospect of learning all of these things can feel very overwhelming for new researchers. After the excitement of starting my PhD faded, I felt extremely lost (the infamous [second-year slump](https://girlymicrobiologist.com/2022/07/04/the-second-year-slump-understanding-the-ups-and-downs-of-doing-a-phd/)), like I knew that I had a mountain of (meta-)skills to develop, but no way to figure out exactly what I needed to learn, let alone where and how to learn them. I was also sort of embarrassed that I didn't already have these skills that seemed like basic prerequisites for doing research — so much so that I avoided asking my advisors or older grad students for help, for fear of them discovering that I was incompetent and an impostor, and had tricked them into recruiting me. 

With the advantage of hindsight, I now know this wasn't true! I was not secretly a fraud, and these feelings weren't even unique to me. Almost every grad student I've talked to has felt, at some point, that they lacked basic knowledge and didn't know **anything** they needed to know. Developing these skills, either implicitly or explicitly, is a natural part of being a researcher-in-training, and (non-asshole) advisors and mentors understand that junior researchers have to juggle research responsibilities with copious amounts of learning.

All this to say, it seems like many junior researchers, including the past version of me, would benefit from a centralized place to figure out what skills they need to develop, how to develop them, and a curated set of guides/resources for doing so. As I've gained more experience doing research, I've also realized that writing notes and documenting workflows makes it way easier to relearn things that you forgot, and work with other people. Because I am benevolent and also forget anything I haven't used in the past month, I made this repo to deposit all the knowledge I have hunted and gathered, and I will continue to add to it as I learn (the many) more things I do not yet know. Hopefully this will allow others to avoid suffering as I have. 

The repo is very much under construction at the moment, but it appears that it will become a conglomeration of explanatory guides and helpful external resources for meta-skills, opinionated standard procedures for doing and disseminating research, my notes on advanced/niche technical skills (e.g. using JAX), and cheatsheets for when you forget a shortcut or command.

Three caveats: 
1. This is by no means exhaustive or fully original, as I do not aspire to replicate all relevant knowledge. The documents here are meant more as guides/summaries than exhaustive manuals. I reference more in-depth resources and actual manuals heavily (when they exist)!
2. The specific apps/workflows/conventions/shortcuts listed are what works for me, but are by no means the optimal choice for everyone. If you prefer other things, you can fork this repo and keep what you want! If you think there's a big gap or I'm using something very obviously inferior, tell me (or open an issue, or better yet, dump your knowledge and make a pull request)!
3. **(Anti-LLM rant)** I enjoy doing the intellectual labor of research and I don't think conceptual understanding is cleanly separable from low-level implementation. Especially for things you are new to, actually doing things, experiencing failure, and gradually building up your knowledge is a long-term investment in your competence (and also personally rewarding). So for most topics here, there's a conspicuous lack of information on how to integrate LLMs and agents, since I believe that junior researchers should avoid them like the plague. The few exceptions are for feedback on things you have *already written* and as a search engine, where natural language understanding is very helpful. Many rote tasks are automatable in a deterministic way *after you know what you are doing*.

## Organization
The basic organization of the repo is as follows: Each subfolder also has its own README with more details!
- `aaaaa-help`: how to use this repo, recommended sequences for acquiring skills, actionable advice for practicing and improving
- `cheatsheets`: quick reference sheets on syntax/commands for commonly used tools and coding languages. These are super utilitarian so there's very minimal explanations.
- `code-management`: guides for coding-related meta-skills, and standard procedures for organizing and maintaining code. 
- `command-line`: fundamentals for the command-line interface and guides/notes on specific shells or CLI tools.
- `shortcuts-and-snippets`: lists of keybinds, macros, and snippets for apps and editors.
- `writing`: guidance and protocols for writing and making figures about your work, relevant style guides, and links to good examples of scientific writing.
